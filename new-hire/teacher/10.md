# Teacher notes, stop 10: Timing a path

## What the student should leave with

- Path against trajectory, and that turning one into the other is a step with
  settings of its own.
- Why a corner is either a stop or a blend, and what a blend costs in straying.
- Speed along a path: one speedometer, with cornering and speeding up sharing the
  motors; the backward and forward passes.
- How `max_deviation` in radians is tied to a margin in millimetres.
- That "is blending quicker?" depends on what it is compared with.

Use the car out loud: the road is the path, the speedometer the path speed, grip the
acceleration limit.

simulated or computed times, so they should repeat exactly.

1. `path_timing_test --test_output=all`, "one corner between two 0.5 rad legs":

   | Turn, degrees | At 0.002 rad | At 0.02 rad | Stopping at it | Straight, same length |
   |---|---|---|---|---|
   | 2 | 1.526 | 1.524 | 2.218 | 1.524 |
   | 10 | 1.888 | 1.801 | 2.211 | 1.524 |
   | 30 | 2.149 | 1.773 | 2.149 | 1.524 |
   | 60 | 2.385 | 2.287 | 2.385 | 1.524 |
   | 90 | 2.472 | 2.419 | 2.472 | 1.524 |

   At 0.002 rad a right angle is exactly as quick rounded as stopped: the planner
   tried the blend, found it no quicker, and stopped. Rounding pays at 2 and 10
   degrees and has stopped paying by 30. With ten times the deviation it still pays
   at 30 degrees and barely at 90. The other line: "31 waypoints on a gentle curve:
   1.629 s in 5 stretch(es); stopping at each 9.693 s; ... straight for the same
   length 1.145 s", the header's figures.
2. `path_follow_test --test_output=all`, "pull back, tool level, no module, 0.3 m in
   31 waypoints", measured in the simulated arm:

   | Way | Time | Tip off the line |
   |---|---|---|
   | stop every centimetre (`MoveThrough`) | 9.27 s | 0.041 mm |
   | `MoveAlong`, 0.002 rad | 1.30 s | 0.456 mm |
   | `MoveAlong`, 0.0002 rad | 1.53 s | 0.069 mm |
   | one joint move, end to end | 1.11 s | 8.868 mm |

   The quickest is the one that ignores the line. Blending is the way to be nearly as
   quick as that and nearly as straight as stopping.
3. `blend_lines = false`: the cycle is quicker, 31.93 s against 32.78 s, with the same
   placement (0.10 mm along, 0.14 mm across). Approach 1.20 s against 1.54, lift 1.54
   against 1.74, pull back 1.92 against 2.20, clear 1.51 against 1.86; lower is
   slower, 2.36 against 1.97. Without blending the script does not stop every
   centimetre: it keeps only the ends of a few legs and makes one joint move per leg
   (the comment at the top of `src/pick_place.h`, "The straight moves"). That is the
   fourth row of lab 2: quick, and off the line by millimetres to centimetres. Lower
   is slower because its way in is cut in two with a stop between
   (`PickPlaceOptions::in_legs`). In this cycle blending buys straightness, for 0.85 s.
4. `deviation_near = 2e-3`: cycle 32.08 s, 0.70 s quicker. Lift 1.54 s (from 1.74),
   pull back 1.92 (2.20), set down 0.76 (0.86), retreat 1.04 (1.16); the other steps
   and the placement unchanged. On "would you ship it": one nominal pallet is not
   evidence. The tight value keeps the tip within about 0.06 mm of the line at the
   glass and the rails, against about 0.5 mm; whether that matters shows on offset
   pallets with miscalibrated cameras, which the gate of stop 12 runs. And the choice
   is the owner's.
5. `print_plan --pretty --seeds 5`: trajectory median 6.19 s, worst 7.02 s. With
   `--corner 0` (first line: "planning margin 40.0 mm ... max deviation 0.00 mrad"):

   ways (5.26 to 6.16, 6.19 to 5.55) because the margin, and so the path, changed. A
   shortened carry is a taut string with a few real corners, and the waypoints added
   along its straight runs are collinear and cost nothing ("collinear, 4 legs: 1.490
   s; straight 1.490 s" in lab 1's output). Lab 1's gentle curve is the opposite.

## Questions to ask

1. *Explain.* Give one path and one trajectory from the code, and say what the second
   has that the first lacks.
   A good answer covers: `JointPath` against `Trajectory` (or `JointMove`);
   `TimedMove` holds both; time, and with it speed, acceleration and jerk.
2. *Predict.* (Before lab 1.) A right-angle corner, allowed to stray 0.002 rad. How
   much quicker is rounding it than stopping?
   A good answer after the run covers: no quicker; `l` is about 0.011 rad, a bend so
   tight it must be crawled through; the code compares and stops instead.
   If stuck, point at: "Stop or blend, corner by corner" in the header.
3. *Explain.* The backward and the forward pass, as if driving.
   A good answer covers: backward is braking distance, how fast may I be here and
   still make everything ahead; forward is how fast I can have got here from rest;
   take the smaller. If stuck, point at: `Time` in `src/path_timing.cc`.
4. *Explain.* The planner allowed 5 mm for corners and timed the carry with 0.60 mrad.
   Where does the second number come from, and why is it safe?
   A good answer covers: 5 mm divided by the length of the lever-arm vector, about
   8.3 m; a path within `d` of the polyline moves no point more than `|r| * d`; it is
   a worst case (the header measured about 0.5 mm at the tip for 0.002 rad, against a
   bound of 15 mm).
5. *Predict.* (Before lab 3.) Switch blending off in the cycle: slower or quicker?
   A good answer after the run covers: quicker, because the alternative in the script
   is one joint move per leg, not a stop every centimetre; what was given up is the
   straight line.
6. *Pitfall.* A time parameterization rounds corners within 0.01 rad, and the arm
   carries a part whose lever arms have a length of about 8 m. How far can the part's
   corner be from the path that was checked for collisions?
   A good answer covers: up to about 80 mm in the worst case; compare with the
   padding; either shrink the tolerance, grow the padding, or check the timed path.
7. *Pitfall.* What is wrong with
   `const Segment& last = segments.back(); segments.push_back(s); use(last);`?
   A good answer covers: `push_back` may move the vector's storage; `last` then
   refers to freed memory; it may work for a long time before it fails. Finish with
   the reference first, as `append` does, or take a copy.
8. *Transfer.* What does MoveIt call this step, and where do its acceleration limits
   come from?
   A good answer covers: time parameterization, an adapter after the planner; a
   `joint_limits.yaml`, because a URDF has only velocity limits.

## Misconceptions to listen for

- "Blending always saves time." Labs 1 and 3 each show a case where it does not.
- "The trajectory goes through the waypoints." Only the first and the last, exactly.
- "Half speed means half the acceleration." A quarter, and an eighth of the jerk.
- "Timing cannot cause a collision." It can, by leaving the checked polyline, unless
  the margin was sized for it.
- "It is time-optimal." Near; the header lists the four reasons it is not.

## Questions the student may ask

- *Why this blend and not a circular arc?* The direction turns slowly at first and at
  the end, so the curvature is zero where the blend meets the straight parts (the
  header's part 1). An arc's curvature starts abruptly: a jump in acceleration, which
  a jerk limit forbids. Kunz and Stilman use arcs; their method has no jerk limit.
- *Why not use Ruckig or TOPP-RA?* Not decided. `docs/ompl-integration.md` looks at
  what bringing Ruckig in would take and notes that its open version does not handle
  intermediate waypoints. A good entry for the notebook's ideas list.
